\title{Water hammer in domestic hot water pipes}
\author{The physics of pressure surges, and how the waterhammer model decides whether a pipe needs protection}
\date{4 October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent When a tap or an appliance valve shuts, the water flowing in the
pipe behind it has to stop. Its momentum does not vanish: it becomes a pressure
wave that runs back and forth along the pipe, and the pressure at the valve can
rise by many times the normal supply pressure. This is \emph{water hammer}.
This note sets out the physics the waterhammer model uses to decide whether a
domestic hot water branch needs protection against it: how fast the wave
travels, how large the surge is for fast and slow valves, how the model
simulates the transient, when the water column can break apart, and how large a
gas-cushion arrester has to be. It ends with the acceptance limits, a worked
example and screening results. The Python package and the Excel workbook
implement exactly these equations, and agree with each other to machine
precision.
\end{abstract}

\section{The setting}

Consider a straight, horizontal pipe of length $L$ (m) with a circular bore of
inside diameter $D$ (m) and a wall of thickness $e$ (m). The bore's
cross-sectional area is $A=\pi D^2/4$ (m$^2$). One end of the pipe joins a
point where the pressure stays nearly constant whatever happens in the pipe: a
much larger main, a manifold, a hot water cylinder or an expansion vessel. Call
this the \emph{reflection point}. The other end has a valve: a tap, a shower
valve or an appliance's solenoid valve.

Water of density $\rho$ (kg/m$^3$) flows through the pipe at a volume flow rate
$Q$ (m$^3$/s), so its mean velocity is $v=Q/A$ (m/s).\sidenote{At 6\lpm{},
$Q=10^{-4}$\,m$^3$/s. In 10\,mm copper (bore 8.8\,mm, $A=6.1\times10^{-5}$\,m$^2$)
that is $v=1.64$\,m/s.} With no flow, the gauge pressure everywhere in the
pipe is the \emph{rest pressure} $p_s$ (Pa), the pressure the supply holds at
the reflection point. In this note a gauge pressure is measured relative to the
atmosphere, and $1\text{ bar}=10^5$\,Pa.

The setting is a domestic hot water branch: flows of 1 to 6\lpm{}, pipes of
about 10 to 15\,mm outside diameter in copper, PE-X (cross-linked
polyethylene), PB (polybutylene) or multilayer composite (MLCP), rest pressures
of 2.5 to 4\,bar and water at 50 to 60\degC{}.

\section{The mechanism}

When the valve shuts, the layer of water touching it stops first. Its pressure
rises, which compresses that water slightly and stretches the pipe wall around
it. The next layer upstream then meets stationary water and stops in turn. A
front of raised pressure, with stationary water behind it, so runs upstream at
the \emph{wave speed} $a$ (m/s). As the section on wave speed shows, $a$ is
close to the speed of sound in water for copper and much lower for plastic.

The front reaches the reflection point after a time $L/a$. At that moment all
the water in the pipe is at rest and above the rest pressure, while the
reflection point is still at $p_s$. Water therefore starts to flow back out of
the pipe, and a wave that restores the pressure to $p_s$ travels down to the
valve. It arrives at time
\begin{equation}
  T_c = \frac{2L}{a},
  \label{eq:tc}
\end{equation}
where $T_c$ (s) is the \emph{critical time}.\sidenote{A 10\,m copper run has
$T_c\approx15$\,ms; the same run in PE-X has $T_c\approx70$\,ms.} Because the
water is now moving away from the closed valve, the pressure there falls below
$p_s$, and a low-pressure wave goes back upstream. With no friction the cycle
would repeat for ever, the pressure at the valve swinging above and below $p_s$
with period $4L/a$. Friction damps it, and in real installations so do trapped
air, flexible hoses and fittings that move.

\section{Surge from a fast closure}

Let $\Delta v$ (m/s) be the drop in velocity across the wave front; for a valve
that shuts completely, $\Delta v=v$. Let $\Delta p$ (Pa) be the rise in
pressure across the front.

Take a short length of pipe around the front. Relative to the pipe, the front
moves upstream at $a-v$, while the water ahead of it moves downstream at $v$, so
the front meets new water at the relative speed $a$. In a short time $\delta t$
(s) it therefore brings a mass $\rho A a\,\delta t$ (kg) of water to a stop
(strictly, from $v$ to $v-\Delta v$). Newton's second law says that the
impulse of the net force equals the change of momentum. The net force is the
pressure difference $\Delta p$ acting on the area $A$, so
\begin{equation}
  \Delta p\,A\,\delta t = \left(\rho A a\,\delta t\right)\Delta v
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \Delta p = \rho\,a\,\Delta v .
  \label{eq:joukowsky}
\end{equation}
This is Joukowsky's equation.\sidecite{joukowsky1904} It gives the full surge
whenever the valve stops the flow before the first returning wave can reach it,
that is when the valve's effective closure time $t_v$ (s) is no more than
$T_c$. The \emph{effective} closure time is the time over which the valve
actually throttles the flow, usually only the last part of the handle's
travel. The surge depends neither on the length of the pipe nor on the
pressure already in it.\sidenote{In copper, $\rho a\approx13$\,bar per m/s.
A flow of 1\,m/s stopped instantly gives 13\,bar, more than six times the
2\,bar limit set out under Acceptance limits.}

\section{Wave speed}
\label{sec:wavespeed}

The wave speed follows from conservation of mass across the front. Water keeps
arriving at the front at velocity $v$ but leaves it at $v-\Delta v$, so in time
$\delta t$ a volume $A\,\Delta v\,\delta t$ of extra water has to be stored in
the length $a\,\delta t$ the front has swept. There are two places to store it.
The water is compressed: its density rises by a fraction $\Delta p/K$, where $K$
(Pa) is the water's \emph{bulk modulus}. And the pipe swells: its bore area
rises by a fraction $\Delta A/A$. So
\begin{equation}
  A\,\Delta v\,\delta t = A\,a\,\delta t\left(\frac{\Delta p}{K}+\frac{\Delta A}{A}\right).
  \label{eq:mass}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{How much the pipe swells.} The pressure rise $\Delta p$ adds a hoop
(circumferential) stress $\Delta p\,D/(2e)$ (Pa) to a thin wall. If $E$ (Pa) is
the wall material's Young's modulus, the hoop strain, the fractional increase in
diameter, is $\Delta p\,D/(2eE)$, and the area grows by twice that fraction:
$\Delta A/A=\Delta p\,D/(eE)$. Real pipes are not quite thin, and how the pipe
is held lengthwise changes the answer slightly, because a pipe that cannot
lengthen develops an axial stress as it swells. Both effects are carried by a
dimensionless \emph{restraint factor} $\psi$, so that
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\Delta A}{A}=\psi\,\frac{\Delta p\,D}{E\,e}.
\end{equation}
For a thick-walled pipe whose material has Poisson's ratio $\nu$ (dimensionless:
the lateral contraction per unit of stretch),\sidecite[\S2.4]{wylie1993}
\begin{equation}
  \psi = 2\,\frac{e}{D}\,(1+\nu) + \frac{D}{D+e}\,\phi,
  \label{eq:psi}
\end{equation}
where $\phi=1-\nu^2$ for a pipe anchored against lengthwise movement throughout
(clipped at short intervals, or buried in a screed), $\phi=1$ for a pipe free to
move lengthwise, and $\phi=1-\nu/2$ for a pipe anchored only at its upstream
end.\sidenote{As $e/D\to0$ the first term vanishes and $D/(D+e)\to1$, so a thin
pipe free to move has $\psi=1$, the textbook form.}

\paragraph{The result.} Substituting $\Delta v=\Delta p/(\rho a)$ from
Equation~\ref{eq:joukowsky} into Equation~\ref{eq:mass} and cancelling
$A\,\delta t\,\Delta p$ gives $1/(\rho a)=a\left(1/K+\psi D/(Ee)\right)$, which
rearranges to the Korteweg formula
\begin{equation}
  a = \sqrt{\frac{K/\rho}{1+\psi\,\dfrac{K D}{E e}}}\,.
  \label{eq:wavespeed}
\end{equation}
In a rigid pipe ($E\to\infty$), $a$ becomes $\sqrt{K/\rho}$, the speed of sound
in water: about 1500\,m/s. Copper ($E\approx117$\,GPa) is stiff enough to stay
close to that. Plastics are more than a hundred times more flexible, so the
pipe term dominates and $a$ falls to a few hundred metres per second
(Figure~\ref{fig:wavespeed}).

\paragraph{Properties used.} For water, $K=\rho c^2$, where $c$ (m/s) is the
speed of sound in water; $\rho$ and $c$ come from the IAPWS-95 formulation as
functions of temperature.\sidecite{iapws95} $K$ is largest near 50\degC{}, so
hot water gives a slightly \emph{higher} surge than cold water in a copper
pipe. For plastics, $E$ has to be the short-term (dynamic) modulus, because a
surge loads the wall for milliseconds, not years. It falls steeply with
temperature: for PE-X the model uses 0.70\,GPa at 20\degC{} and 0.42\,GPa at
60\degC{}. These are indicative values, and a manufacturer's figure should
replace them where one exists.

\section{Slower closure}

If the valve takes longer than $T_c$ to shut, waves returning from the
reflection point arrive while it is still closing and cancel part of the rise.

\paragraph{Michaud's formula.} Suppose the velocity at the valve falls at a
steady rate, from $v$ to zero over $t_v$. Each instant of closure sends a small
Joukowsky step up the pipe, so the pressure at the valve climbs in proportion
to the velocity lost so far, $\rho a\,v\,t/t_v$ at time $t$ (s) after closure
starts. At $t=T_c$ the first returning wave arrives and the climb stops: from
then on the steps still being sent are cancelled by those coming back. The peak
is therefore the Joukowsky surge scaled by $T_c/t_v$:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta p_{\max} = \rho a \Delta v\,\frac{T_c}{t_v} = \frac{2\rho L\,\Delta v}{t_v}
  \qquad (t_v > T_c).
  \label{eq:michaud}
\end{equation}
The wave speed has cancelled. For slow closures the surge depends only on the
run length, the velocity and the closure time, not on the pipe
material.\sidenote{For a steady fall in velocity in a frictionless pipe,
Equation~\ref{eq:michaud} is the exact peak. The pressure then oscillates
between $p_s$ and this peak, about a mean of $\rho L\Delta v/t_v$, which is what
treating the water as a rigid column predicts.}

\paragraph{Real valves.} A tap discharging to atmosphere does not reduce the
velocity at a steady rate. Its flow obeys the orifice law
\begin{equation}
  Q = \tau(t)\,Q_0\sqrt{H_v/H_{v0}},
  \label{eq:orifice}
\end{equation}
where $\tau$ (dimensionless) is the fraction of the valve's opening that is
still open, $Q_0$ (m$^3$/s) is the steady flow before closure, and $H_v$ (m) is
the gauge pressure just upstream of the valve expressed as a height of water,
$H_v=p/(\rho g)$, with $p$ (Pa) that gauge pressure and $g=9.81$\,m/s$^2$ the
acceleration due to gravity. $H_{v0}$ is its steady value while the water
flows. With $\tau$ falling at a steady rate, most of the flow is choked only
near the end of the stroke, which is why the effective closure time is shorter
than the handle's travel. When $t_v\gg T_c$ the water behaves as a rigid column,
and Newton's law for the whole column gives
\begin{equation}
  \frac{L}{gA}\frac{\dd Q}{\dd t} = H_R - h_f(Q) - H_v,
  \label{eq:rigid}
\end{equation}
where $H_R=p_s/(\rho g)$ (m) is the head at the reflection point and $h_f$ (m)
the head lost to friction along the pipe at flow $Q$. The model uses this limit
to check its simulation.

\section{Simulating the transient}

To handle any valve and closure time, the model solves the one-dimensional
water hammer equations.\sidecite[ch.~3]{wylie1993} Let $x$ (m) be the distance
along the pipe from the reflection point, $t$ (s) time, and $H(x,t)$ (m) and
$Q(x,t)$ (m$^3$/s) the local pressure head and flow. Mass and momentum
conservation give
\begin{align}
  \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} + \frac{a^2}{gA}\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} &= 0, \\
  \frac{\partial Q}{\partial t} + gA\frac{\partial H}{\partial x} + \frac{f\,Q|Q|}{2DA} &= 0,
\end{align}
where $f$ (dimensionless) is the Darcy friction factor. The model takes
$f=64/Re$ for laminar flow ($Re<2300$) and the Swamee--Jain formula
$f=0.25\big/\left[\log_{10}\!\left(\varepsilon/3.7D+5.74/Re^{0.9}\right)\right]^2$
above that. Here $Re=\rho vD/\mu$ is the Reynolds number (dimensionless), $\mu$
(Pa\,s) the water's dynamic viscosity and $\varepsilon$ (m) the roughness of the
bore. The friction head over the whole pipe is then
$h_f=f\,(L/D)\,v^2/(2g)$.

\paragraph{The method of characteristics.} Along lines in the $(x,t)$ plane
whose slope is $\dd x/\dd t=\pm a$, the two partial differential equations
reduce to ordinary ones. Divide the pipe into $N$ equal reaches of length
$\Delta x=L/N$ (m), and step time by $\Delta t=\Delta x/a$ (s), so that the
characteristic lines join neighbouring grid points. Write $H_i$ and $Q_i$ for
the values at node $i$ (node $0$ is the reflection point, node $N$ the valve) at
the previous time step, and $H_P$, $Q_P$ for the new values at node $i$. Then
\begin{align}
  C^+:&\quad H_P = C_P - B\,Q_P, & C_P &= H_{i-1} + B\,Q_{i-1} - R\,Q_{i-1}|Q_{i-1}|, \\
  C^-:&\quad H_P = C_M + B\,Q_P, & C_M &= H_{i+1} - B\,Q_{i+1} + R\,Q_{i+1}|Q_{i+1}|,
\end{align}
where $B=a/(gA)$ (s/m$^2$) is the pipe's characteristic impedance and
$R=f\,\Delta x/(2gDA^2)$ (s$^2$/m$^5$) its friction coefficient per reach.
Solving the pair gives, at each interior node,
$H_P=(C_P+C_M)/2$ and $Q_P=(C_P-C_M)/(2B)$.

\paragraph{Ends of the pipe.} At the reflection point the head is fixed,
$H_P=H_R$, so the $C^-$ equation gives $Q_P=(H_R-C_M)/B$. At the valve, the
$C^+$ equation and the orifice law (Equation~\ref{eq:orifice}) together give a
quadratic in $Q_P$, whose positive root is
\begin{equation}
  Q_P = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(-kB + \sqrt{k^2B^2 + 4k\,C_P}\right),
  \qquad k = \frac{(\tau Q_0)^2}{H_{v0}}\ \ (\text{m}^5/\text{s}^2),
\end{equation}
with $Q_P=0$ once the valve is shut. The open fraction falls at a steady rate,
$\tau=1-t/t_v$. The simulation starts from steady flow, with the head falling
linearly from $H_R$ at the reflection point to $H_{v0}=H_R-h_f$ at the valve,
and runs until six periods of $2L/a$ after the valve has shut. The tests check
that it reproduces Joukowsky's surge for an instantaneous closure (within 3\%)
and the rigid-column result of Equation~\ref{eq:rigid} for slow closures
(within 5\%).

Figure~\ref{fig:closure} shows the outcome. Below $t_v\approx T_c$ every closure
gives the full Joukowsky surge. Above it the surge falls roughly as $1/t_v$. The
simulated curve can lie above or below Michaud's line, depending on how hard the
orifice throttles the flow compared with the rest pressure.\sidenote{Allievi's
constant, $a\,v/(2gH_{v0})$ (dimensionless), measures this: the ratio of the
Joukowsky head rise to twice the head at the valve. In the two examples it is
about 4 for copper and 1 for PE-X.} The model therefore takes the
\emph{larger} of the formula and the simulation as the design surge, written
$\Delta p_d$ (Pa).

\section{Column separation}

Each high-pressure wave is followed by a low-pressure one of about the same size
(Figure~\ref{fig:trace}). If the absolute pressure anywhere falls to the water's
\emph{vapour pressure} $p_{vap}$ (Pa), the pressure at which water boils at its
current temperature, the column breaks and a vapour cavity forms. When the
columns rejoin, the impact can exceed Joukowsky's surge.\sidecite[ch.~8]{wylie1993}
The model detects this but does not simulate it: any minimum pressure below
$p_{vap}$ counts as a failure.\sidenote{At 55\degC{}, $p_{vap}=0.16$\,bar
absolute, or $-0.85$\,bar gauge. With a rest pressure of 3\,bar the column
separates once the surge exceeds about 3.9\,bar.}

\section{Gas-cushion arresters}

A water hammer arrester puts a volume of gas, kept apart from the water by a
piston or membrane, next to the valve. To a first approximation it must absorb
the kinetic energy of the moving column, $\tfrac12\rho A L v^2$ (J), while the
pressure rises by no more than an allowed amount $\Delta p_a$ (Pa). Let $p_0$
(Pa, absolute) be the gas's precharge pressure and $V_0$ (m$^3$) its volume at
that pressure, and suppose the gas is compressed polytropically, so that
$p V^n$ stays constant with index $n$ (dimensionless; $n=1$ for slow,
isothermal compression and $n=1.4$ for fast, adiabatic compression of air or
nitrogen). The work done on the gas in compressing it from $p_0$ to
$p_0+\Delta p_a$ then equals the kinetic energy:
\begin{equation}
  \tfrac12 \rho A L v^2 = \frac{p_0 V_0}{n-1}\left[\left(\frac{p_0+\Delta p_a}{p_0}\right)^{(n-1)/n} - 1\right],
  \label{eq:arrester}
\end{equation}
which tends to $p_0V_0\ln\!\left[(p_0+\Delta p_a)/p_0\right]$ as $n\to1$.
Solving for $V_0$ gives the precharge volume. The model takes $n=1.4$, which
needs more gas than $n=1$, and sets $p_0$ to the rest pressure plus
atmospheric pressure. It ignores the energy taken up by the stretching pipe and
the compressed water, which makes the estimate conservative. The result shows
the scale needed; the product itself should be chosen from the manufacturer's
data, or by PDI-WH~201 in US practice.\sidecite{pdiwh201}

UK and European practice sets a performance requirement: keep the pressure rise
caused by operating a valve within 2\,bar \citep{en806,din1988,vdi6006}. The UK
Water Fittings Regulations also require fittings to resist pressure surges and
to withstand 1.5 times the maximum pressure they are designed to operate at
\citep{wsr1999}. US codes are prescriptive instead: the International Plumbing
Code and the Uniform Plumbing Code require an arrester certified to ASSE~1010
wherever quick-closing valves are used \citep{ipc,upc,asse1010}.
Table~\ref{tab:limits} lists the checks.

When mitigation is needed the model offers three remedies. The first is an
arrester at the valve, sized by Equation~\ref{eq:arrester} with
$\Delta p_a$ the smaller of the surge limit and the margin below the pipe
rating. The second is the shortest effective closure time that meets the
limit. The Python model finds it by bisection on the larger of Michaud's
formula and the simulation; the Excel workbook gives Michaud's estimate,
$t_v=2\rho Lv/\Delta p_a$. The third is the largest flow at which even an
instantaneous closure passes, from Equation~\ref{eq:joukowsky}:
$v\le\Delta p_a/(\rho a)$.

\section{Worked example}

effective $t_v=20$\,ms at the end of $L=8$\,m of copper pipe, 10\,mm outside
diameter with a 0.6\,mm wall. The flow is 6\lpm{} of water at 55\degC{}, and
the rest pressure is 3\,bar.

\section{What the screening shows}

Figure~\ref{fig:screening} applies the model across the project's design range.
Solenoid valves on small-bore pipe exceed the limit at almost any length once
the flow passes 2 to 3\lpm{}. Lever mixers and quarter-turn taps are acceptable
on short runs but fail on long small-bore ones. Reducing the bore to cut the
volume of a dead leg raises the velocity, and so the surge. The two design aims
pull against each other, and this model is the check on that trade-off.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One pipe, one valve.} Local losses at fittings, branches and
    unsteady friction are left out. A branched network needs a full transient
    analysis.\sidecite{chaudhry2014}
  \item \textbf{Closure times.} The valve closure times are engineering
    estimates. Where a result is marginal, the valve should be measured.
  \item \textbf{Plastic moduli.} These are indicative, and multilayer pipe is
    treated as a single homogenised wall.
  \item \textbf{Column separation} is detected, not simulated.
  \item \textbf{Softening.} Trapped air and flexible connections soften real
    surges, so the results lean conservative.
\end{itemize}
