\begin{abstract}
\noindent When a hot tap is opened at the end of a pipe that has gone cold, the
first water out is the cold water that was standing in the pipe. That much is
simple displacement. But the hot water that follows has to heat the pipe wall
as it goes, and it loses a little heat to the air. So the tap runs warm later,
and more water is wasted, than displacement alone suggests. This note sets out
the physics of that transient: the governing equations, the few
dimensionless numbers that control it, the analytical limits, and why copper and
plastic pipes behave so differently. The model built on it predicts the delivery
times of 46 field tests to within a few seconds, with no fitted constants.
\end{abstract}

\section{The question}

A pipe of length $L$ and bore radius $r_i$ holds a volume $V=\pi r_i^2L$ of
water. If hot water simply pushed the cold water out, like a piston, the hot
water would reach the tap after the \emph{plug-flow time}
\begin{equation}
  t_{\text{plug}} = \frac{V}{Q} = \frac{L}{u},
  \qquad u = \frac{Q}{\pi r_i^2},
  \label{eq:plug}
\end{equation}
where $Q$ is the volume flow and $u$ the mean velocity.\sidenote{For 5\,m of
10\,mm copper (bore 8.8\,mm), $V=0.30$\,l and $t_{\text{plug}}=6$\,s at
3\lpm{}. At 1\lpm{} it is 18\,s.} Everything else in this note explains why the
real delivery time is longer than this, and by how much.

The setting is a domestic hot water branch: flows of 1 to 6\lpm{} in pipes of
roughly 10 to 12\,mm outside diameter, made of copper or plastic (PE-X). At the
start, the water in the pipe, the pipe wall and the surrounding air are all at
the same temperature $T_0$, typically about 20\degC{}. At $t=0$ the tap opens and
water arrives at the inlet at $T_{in}$, for example 55\degC{}.

The model follows four bodies (Figure~\ref{fig:section}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{the water}, flowing along the pipe as a plug, with a bulk
    temperature $T_w(x,t)$ that varies along the pipe;
  \item \textbf{the pipe wall}, homogeneous and axially symmetric, with a
    temperature $T_p(r,x,t)$ that varies through the thickness and along the length;
  \item \textbf{the air}, a single well-mixed body at $T_a(t)$ that warms as the
    pipe gives it heat;
  \item optionally, \textbf{an environment} at a fixed temperature, to which the
    air body can lose heat.
\end{enumerate}
They exchange heat in a chain: water to wall by forced convection, through the
wall by conduction, and wall to air by natural convection and radiation.

\section{Governing equations}

\paragraph{Water.} An energy balance on a thin slice of water $\dd x$, carried
along with the flow, gives
\begin{equation}
  \rho_w c_w A_i\left(\frac{\partial T_w}{\partial t}+u\frac{\partial T_w}{\partial x}\right)
  = h_i\, 2\pi r_i\,\bigl(T_p(r_i)-T_w\bigr),
  \label{eq:water}
\end{equation}
with $A_i=\pi r_i^2$, the inlet condition $T_w(0,t)=T_{in}(t)$ and the initial
condition $T_w(x,0)=T_0$. The left side is the rate of change of the heat held
by the slice, seen by an observer moving with the water. The right side is the
heat it gives to the wall.\sidenote{Axial conduction in the water is neglected.
The Péclet number $uL/\alpha_w$ is about $10^7$, so heat is carried along the
pipe by the flow and hardly at all by conduction.}

\paragraph{Wall.} Transient conduction in an axially symmetric annulus,
\begin{equation}
  \rho_p c_p \frac{\partial T_p}{\partial t}
  = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(k_p r \frac{\partial T_p}{\partial r}\right)
  + k_p\frac{\partial^2 T_p}{\partial x^2},
  \qquad r_i<r<r_o ,
  \label{eq:wall}
\end{equation}
with convective conditions on both faces,
\begin{align}
  -k_p\frac{\partial T_p}{\partial r}\Big|_{r_i} &= h_i\bigl(T_w-T_p(r_i)\bigr), \\
  -k_p\frac{\partial T_p}{\partial r}\Big|_{r_o} &= h_o\bigl(T_p(r_o)-T_a\bigr).
\end{align}
The wall starts at $T_0$, and its ends are taken as insulated.

\paragraph{Air.} The air body has a single temperature, so its balance is an
ordinary differential equation:
\begin{equation}
  \rho_a c_a V_a \frac{\dd T_a}{\dd t}
  = \int_0^L h_o\,2\pi r_o\bigl(T_p(r_o)-T_a\bigr)\,\dd x \;-\; UA_{env}\bigl(T_a-T_{env}\bigr).
  \label{eq:air}
\end{equation}
Letting $V_a\to\infty$ holds the air at $T_0$, which represents a large room.

\paragraph{Conservation.} Adding the three equations and integrating over time
gives the energy budget the numerical solution must respect:
\begin{multline}
  \int_0^t \rho_w c_w Q\,(T_{in}-T_{out})\,\dd t'
  = \Delta E_{\text{water}}+\Delta E_{\text{wall}}+\Delta E_{\text{air}} \\
  + \int_0^t UA_{env}(T_a-T_{env})\,\dd t' .
\end{multline}
In words, the heat that entered and did not leave at the tap is now in the
water, the wall or the air, or has gone to the environment.

\section{What controls the delay}

Scaling equations \eqref{eq:water}--\eqref{eq:air} shows that three groups do
almost all the work.

\paragraph{The heat capacity ratio.}
\begin{equation}
  \sigma = \frac{\rho_p c_p A_p}{\rho_w c_w A_i}
\end{equation}
is the heat the wall can absorb per metre for each degree it warms, relative to
the water's. Suppose the wall took up heat instantly, with no losses. Each
metre of pipe would then have to absorb $(1+\sigma)$ times the heat the water
alone holds before it could pass hot water on. The hot front would therefore
move at a speed of $u/(1+\sigma)$ and arrive at
\begin{equation}
  t_{\text{front}} = (1+\sigma)\,t_{\text{plug}} .
  \label{eq:front}
\end{equation}
This is the most useful single result in this note. Copper's thin wall gives
$\sigma\approx0.2$; plastic's thick wall gives $\sigma\approx0.5$ to 0.6
(Table~\ref{tab:groups}).\sidenote{Per unit volume, copper and PE-X store
heat in a ratio of only about 1.7 to~1. The difference in $\sigma$ comes from
the walls: a plastic wall is two to three times as thick.}

\paragraph{The number of transfer units.}
\begin{equation}
  NTU = \frac{h_i\,2\pi r_i L}{\rho_w c_w Q}
\end{equation}
measures how completely the water hands its heat to the wall in one pass. If it
were very large, the water and the inner surface of the wall would sit at the same
temperature everywhere. Over 5\,m of these pipes it is between about 1 and 4.
That is large enough for the wall to take most of the heat from the leading
water, and small enough that some lukewarm water arrives at the plug-flow time,
ahead of the main rise. Above about 2\lpm{}, $NTU$ hardly changes with flow,
because $h_i$ rises almost in proportion to the velocity.

\paragraph{The wall Biot number and diffusion time.}
\begin{equation}
  Bi = \frac{h_i\,\delta}{k_p},\qquad t_{\text{wall}}=\frac{\delta^2}{\alpha_p},
  \qquad \delta = r_o-r_i,\ \alpha_p=\frac{k_p}{\rho_p c_p}
\end{equation}
compare the resistance of the wall to the resistance of the water film, and give the time
heat takes to soak through the wall. This is where copper and plastic part
company:
\begin{itemize}
  \item copper: $Bi\sim0.01$, $t_{\text{wall}}\approx4$\,ms. The wall is isothermal
    through its thickness from the instant the water touches it, so it behaves as
    a simple lumped heat store;
  \item PE-X: $Bi\sim10$ to 60, $t_{\text{wall}}\approx12$ to 20\,s. The wall is
    a poor conductor; while the hot water passes, only a thin inner skin warms.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:radial} shows the consequence. Copper soaks up its share of heat
at once, so the outlet stays cool until the delayed front of
equation~\eqref{eq:front} arrives, then rises steeply. PE-X takes only a skin of
heat at first. Its effective $\sigma$ is small early on and grows as heat
penetrates the wall. So the first warm water arrives close to the plug-flow
time, but the last few degrees wait for the wall to soak through.

\section{Heat transfer coefficients}

\paragraph{Inside.} The film coefficient comes from the Reynolds number
$Re=uD_i/\nu$. Above $Re=10^4$ the model uses Gnielinski's correlation,
\begin{equation}
  Nu = \frac{(f/8)(Re-1000)Pr}{1+12.7\sqrt{f/8}\,(Pr^{2/3}-1)},
  \qquad f=(0.790\ln Re-1.64)^{-2},
\end{equation}
and below $Re=2300$ the fully developed laminar value $Nu=3.66$. Between the two
it interpolates linearly in $Re$, as the VDI Heat Atlas
recommends.\sidenote{At 1--6\lpm{} in 7--11\,mm bores, $Re$ is about
3000--28000, so the flow is transitional at the lowest flows and turbulent
otherwise. Viscosity halves between 20 and 55\degC{}, so the arriving hot water
is more turbulent than the cold water it displaces. The model evaluates
properties at the local water temperature.} This gives $h_i$ of about
$10^3$ to $1.5\times10^4$\,W/m$^2$K.

\paragraph{Outside.} Natural convection from a horizontal cylinder (Churchill
and Chu) plus radiation to surroundings at the air temperature,
$h_{rad}=\varepsilon\sigma_{SB}(T_s^2+T_a^2)(T_s+T_a)$. For a 50\degC{} pipe
in 20\degC{} air, this gives $h_o\approx9$\,W/m$^2$K for tarnished copper
($\varepsilon=0.1$) and $\approx14$\,W/m$^2$K for plastic ($\varepsilon=0.9$).

The outside coefficient is a thousand times smaller than the inside one. While
the tap is running, the pipe loses only a few watts per metre to the air. Over a
draw of a minute or two, almost all of the heat leaving the water goes into the
wall. External losses set the steady outlet temperature and how quickly the
pipe cools between draws, but they barely change the delivery time. The model
confirms this. Enclosing the pipe in a 1.5\,m$\times$0.3\,m air body instead of
an infinite room changes no field-trial prediction by more than 0.34\,s, even
though the air warms by up to 7\,K.

\section{Exact solutions in limiting cases}

Three limits have closed-form solutions. Each one checks the model and sharpens
intuition.

\paragraph{No wall heat capacity.} The outlet steps from $T_0$ to $T_{in}$ at
$t_{\text{plug}}$, equation~\eqref{eq:plug}.

\paragraph{A thin, perfectly conducting wall with no losses.} This is the
Anzelius--Schumann problem from regenerator theory. In terms of
\[
  \xi = \frac{h_i P x}{\rho_w c_w Q},\qquad
  \eta = \frac{h_i P\,(t-x/u)}{\rho_p c_p A_p},\qquad P=2\pi r_i,
\]
the dimensionless water temperature $\theta=(T_w-T_0)/(T_{in}-T_0)$ is, for
$\eta\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
  \theta = e^{-\xi}\Bigl[e^{-\eta}I_0\bigl(2\sqrt{\xi\eta}\bigr)
           +\int_0^\eta e^{-s}I_0\bigl(2\sqrt{\xi s}\bigr)\,\dd s\Bigr],
\end{equation}
and zero before the plug front arrives. At $x=L$, $\xi$ is the $NTU$. The factor
$e^{-\xi}$ is the step of lukewarm water that arrives at the plug-flow time; the
rest of the rise is spread around $t_{\text{front}}$ of
equation~\eqref{eq:front}.

\begin{marginfigure}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{schumann.pdf}
\caption{The numerical model (grey and black) against the exact

\label{fig:schumann}
\end{marginfigure}

\paragraph{Steady state with losses.} Once the wall has warmed, the outlet
settles at
\begin{gather}
  T_{out} = T_a + (T_{in}-T_a)\exp\!\left(-\frac{U'L}{\rho_w c_w Q}\right),\\
  \frac{1}{U'} = \frac{1}{2\pi r_i h_i}+\frac{\ln(r_o/r_i)}{2\pi k_p}+\frac{1}{2\pi r_o h_o}.
\end{gather}
Because $h_o$ is small, the drop is a fraction of a degree over 5\,m.

\section{How the equations are solved}

The pipe is divided into $N_x$ cells along its length (200 by default). Each
cell has one water node and $N_r$ concentric wall shells, and there is a single
air node. Each time step does three things:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Radial heat exchange} between the water, the wall shells and the
    air, solved implicitly. Each cell's water-and-wall system is tridiagonal, and
    its solution is linear in the new air temperature. That lets the air node,
    which couples every cell, be eliminated exactly, so no iteration is needed;
  \item \textbf{Axial conduction} along the wall, also implicit;
  \item \textbf{Advection}: the time step is chosen as $\Delta t=\Delta x/u$, so
    the water moves exactly one cell per step. Transport is exact, and the front
    is not artificially smeared.
\end{enumerate}
The scheme conserves energy to rounding error. It converges to the
Anzelius--Schumann solution at first order: within 0.7\,\% at 200 cells and
0.2\,\% at 800 (Figure~\ref{fig:schumann}).

\section{What the model predicts}

\begin{figure}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{outlet_traces.pdf}
\caption{Outlet temperature after the tap opens: 5\,m run, 3\lpm{}, inlet
55\degC{}, pipe and air initially 20\degC{}.}
\label{fig:traces}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:traces} shows both behaviours. Each copper trace is flat until
$t_{\text{plug}}$, has a small step of lukewarm water, then rises steadily to
about $(1+\sigma)t_{\text{plug}}$ and beyond. The PE-X traces jump almost to
40\degC{} at the plug-flow time, then creep towards the inlet temperature as
heat soaks into the wall.

Three practical consequences follow.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{For copper, waste is fixed in volume.} About 1.4 to 1.5 pipe
    volumes run off before the tap reaches 50\degC{}, whatever the flow. The
    time therefore scales as $1/Q$.
  \item \textbf{For plastic, the answer depends on what ``hot'' means.} The time
    to 50\degC{} can be twice the time to 40\degC{}. At 1\lpm{}, a PE-X pipe is as
    slow to 50\degC{} as copper of the same outside diameter, despite holding
    less water. At 6\lpm{}, it is faster.
  \item \textbf{A delivery time is meaningful only with its threshold and supply
    temperature.} A 45\degC{} target with 50\degC{} supply sits in the slow
    tail for plastic pipe.
\end{itemize}

a degree matters a great deal there.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Plug flow.} Below $Re\approx2300$ the parabolic velocity profile
    brings the first warm water out at about half the plug-flow time and smears
    the front. That matters only near or below 1\lpm{} with cold water.
  \item \textbf{Steady correlations.} A steady-state $h_i$ is applied to a strongly
    transient wall. The entrance region and the moving thermal front both raise
    the true coefficient slightly.
  \item \textbf{A homogeneous wall.} Multilayer pipe is represented by
    volume-averaged properties.
  \item \textbf{What is left out.} Fittings, clips, insulation and the thermal mass
    of the surrounding structure are not modelled. Radiation is taken to go to
    surroundings at the air temperature.
\end{itemize}
